Research

Observing and modeling ice flow

My PhD research focuses on ice flow in blue ice areas. I use a combination of geophysical field observations and simple models to investigate ice flow.

01

Tidewater Glacier Calving

Calving of icebergs from tidewater glaciers inputs significant energy to the surrounding water. Using novel measurements of water velocities from remotely deployed moorings, I investigated the energy flow from a large submarine calving event at Xeitl Sít' (LeConte Glacier) in Alaska.

Through a careful partitioning of potential and kinetic energy, we showed that submarine calving events can accelerate water velocities for several hours, leading to an increase in melting at the glacier terminus. Calving-related feedbacks may be an important contributor to melting from tidewater glaciers.

02

Ice Flow at the Allan Hills

The Allan Hills, Antarctia, is a blue ice area, where ice flows up steep bedrock topography and are exposed to sublimation and wind scour, revealing blue glacier ice. Ice core drilling at the Allan Hills has returned the oldest known ice on Earth (greater than 6 million years in age)!

The very slow ice velocities of the Allan Hills are challenging to observe. I led collection of the first (to my knowledge) repeat phase-sensitive radar data at a blue ice area. Combined with a kinematic model , these data reveal ice flow across a flowline at the Allan Hills.

03

Field Observations

I am very grateful to have had the opportunity to conduct fieldwork in Antarctica. I have spent two field seasons at the (windy!) Allan Hills, and have helped plan geophysical surveys for two more seasons. We have collected extensive GPS measurements , in addition to phase-sensitive and impulse radar measurements.
